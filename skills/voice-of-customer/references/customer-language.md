# Customer language

The exact words buyers use for the problem, the outcome and the alternatives, collected so copy, ads, pages and sales talk can use them. Writing the copy itself is a separate request; this holds what the evidence says and how to collect and use the words safely.

## What the evidence says

- **Plain language converts better, correlationally.** Across 41,000 landing pages and 57 million conversions, pages at a 5th to 7th grade reading level converted at 11.1%, 56% higher than 8th to 9th grade and more than twice the 5.3% of professional-level writing; words of three or more syllables correlated with conversion at -0.24, and the link had strengthened since 2020. Financial services was the one industry where more complex language helped (Unbounce Conversion Benchmark Report, 2024). Customers rarely write at a professional level, so their wording tends to be plainer than marketing's; the report does not test customer phrasing as such.
- **Matching the reader's style helps.** Amazon book reviews whose linguistic style matched the typical style of the book's audience were followed by higher conversion (Ludwig and others, Journal of Marketing, 2013).
- **Concrete beats abstract.** Customer service agents who used concrete words (naming the specific product rather than "that item") raised customer satisfaction and later spending, in lab studies and a retailer's field data (Packard and Berger, Journal of Consumer Research, 2021).
- **Not measured:** the effect of using customers' verbatim phrases in a headline, in a controlled test. Copywriting case studies claiming large lifts from review-mined headlines are single tests without published controls. Treat customer language as a source of candidate copy to A/B test, not as a proven lift.

## Collecting phrases

- Pull them during a [pain points](pain-points.md) or [objections](objections.md) run; nothing extra to buy.
- Keep the **problem words** (how they name the pain before knowing any product: "chasing invoices", "the deciding part, not the cooking"), the **outcome words** (what good looks like: "I stopped chasing invoices almost completely"), the **alternative words** (what they compare against: "Gmail templates and calendar reminders"), and **words to avoid** (jargon buyers never use, or use with scorn).
- A phrase repeated across several authors and sources is a candidate; one striking quote is an example, not a pattern.
- Search volume tells which problem words people type: `seo_get_keyword_metrics` on the candidate phrases (10 credits for up to 100).
- Watch for AI-written posts: their phrasing ("quietly the worst part", "genuinely curious") is not customer language, and Reddit commenters now call it out ([field notes](field-notes.md)).

## Using them

- In research tables, quote verbatim with the link; never edit inside quote marks.
- In public copy, **paraphrase** what came from public posts and never name or identify the author. Reddit, TikTok and Facebook users are private individuals who did not agree to be in an ad.
- A **testimonial** (a quote attributed to a customer) needs that customer's permission and must be real.

## Consent and the law

The FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials (announced 14 August 2024, in force 21 October 2024, 16 CFR Part 465) bans reviews and testimonials that misrepresent who wrote them, including AI-generated ones and ones from people who do not exist, and lets the FTC seek civil penalties. So in US marketing: no composite "customer" quotes, no synthetic personas quoted as customers, no buying reviews conditioned on sentiment, no undisclosed insider reviews. Elsewhere, consumer-protection law (the UK's DMCC Act 2024 among others) bans fake reviews too. Personal-data rules for people found online live in [outbound](../../outbound/SKILL.md).

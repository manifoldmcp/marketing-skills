# Legal

The basics of cold B2B email law in the US, the UK and the EU, and LinkedIn's rules on automation, as they stood in October 2026. This is orientation, not legal advice: the user decides the legal basis and should take advice for a large programme or a regulated sector. Say this once, plainly, whenever a list covers the UK, the EU or Germany.

## The law follows the recipient

Which rules apply depends on where the person receiving the email is, not where the sender or the company's headquarters are. In our own run, 6 of 18 marketing leaders at US-headquartered companies lived in Canada, the UK, Israel, India or Hong Kong ([field notes](field-notes.md)). Read the person's `location` on every row and split the list by regime before sending.

## United States: CAN-SPAM

From the [FTC's compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business):

- **No exception for B2B.** It covers all commercial email, including to business addresses.
- No prior consent is needed. What is required: honest From, Reply-To and routing headers; a subject line that does not deceive; disclosure that the message is an ad; a valid postal address (a street address, a registered PO box or a commercial mailbox); a clear way to opt out.
- Honour opt-outs within 10 business days, at no cost and asking for nothing but the address.
- The sender is responsible for email sent on its behalf, by an agency or a tool.
- Penalties up to $53,088 per violating email (the FTC's figure as of its January 2024 inflation update; check for a newer one).
- State laws add to this (California's on deceptive commercial email, for one); flag them for a US-wide programme.

## United Kingdom: PECR and UK GDPR

- **PECR splits recipients.** A sender may email a corporate body (a company, an LLP, a Scottish partnership, a government body) without consent, but must not hide its identity, must give a valid address for opt-outs, and must keep a "do not email" list. Sole traders and some partnerships count as individuals: they need consent or the soft opt-in (a previous sale or negotiation for a similar product, with an opt-out offered then and in every message). Source: [ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/electronic-mail-marketing/).
- **UK GDPR still covers the employee.** A named work address (jane.smith@company.com) is personal data, so the sender needs a lawful basis, usually legitimate interests, a privacy notice at first contact (who they are, where the data came from, how to object) and an immediate stop when someone objects.
- **The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025** states that direct marketing can rest on legitimate interests, extends the soft opt-in to charities, and was fully in force by June 2026. It also raises PECR's maximum fines towards UK GDPR levels (widely reported; the ICO's PECR guide, under review after the Act, still showed the old £500,000 cap when checked, so confirm before quoting a number).

## European Union

- The GDPR applies to the employee's data as in the UK: legitimate interests, a notice at first contact with the source of the data, the right to object honoured at once, and records kept.
- Whether B2B cold email needs consent is set by each country's ePrivacy law, and they differ. **Germany** requires prior consent for email advertising, B2B included (unfair competition law), so cold email to German recipients is a real legal risk and a frequent source of warning letters. France allows B2B email without consent when it concerns the recipient's job. Check the country before sending; when unsure, treat the EU as consent-first.

## What every cold email should carry

Whatever the market: the sender's real name and company, a postal address, a one-line opt-out ("reply 'no' and I won't write again") or an unsubscribe link, and, for UK and EU recipients, where the address came from or a link to a notice that says so. Keep the source column from [lead data](lead-data.md#personal-data) so the user can answer that question, and a suppression list that outlives every campaign.

## LinkedIn's rules

- LinkedIn's User Agreement bans third-party software that scrapes the site or automates activity on it, including bots, browser plug-ins and extensions; accounts using them can be restricted or shut down ([LinkedIn help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1341387)). Automated connection requests and messages are a risk to the user's own account, not a gamble on a tool's ban rate.
- LinkedIn also caps connection invitations each week and publishes no exact number. Write to people by hand, and spend the invitations on accounts that fit.
- Manifold reads public LinkedIn data and never acts on an account. Posting and commenting strategy is the [linkedin](../../linkedin/SKILL.md) skill.

## Without the connector

All of this applies the same. Ask where the list came from, which countries its people are in, and whether the user has a suppression list, before helping with a send.

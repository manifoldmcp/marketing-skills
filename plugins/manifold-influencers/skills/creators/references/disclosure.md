# Disclosure and the law

What the user and their creators must do so a paid or gifted post is lawful, as it stood in October 2026. This is a summary to brief from, not legal advice; for a regulated product (health claims, finance, alcohol, children) the user needs a lawyer.

## United States: the Endorsement Guides

The FTC's Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255, revised June 2023) and its FAQ for influencers set the standard. The points briefs get wrong:

- **A free product is a material connection.** So is a discount, early access, a commission, a family tie or employment. Repeated freebies create a relationship even without a contract.
- **Disclose in the medium of the endorsement:** said aloud in a video and shown on screen, at the start and repeated in long videos and live streams. A disclosure only in the description, behind a "more" link, at the end of a caption or in the comments is not enough.
- **Platform tools are not automatically sufficient.** The FTC judges whether the label itself is clear; adding the creator's own disclosure is the safer course. Responsibility rests with the influencer and the brand, not the platform.
- **Words that work:** "ad", "#ad", "paid ad", "advertisement", "sponsored by [brand]", "gifted by [brand]" for a free product. **Words that do not:** "sp", "spon", "collab", a stand-alone "thanks" or "ambassador" (FTC, Disclosures 101), and #partner, #comped, #hosted, #freeproduct, "gifted" without the brand, "ad" buried inside another word (Endorsement Guides FAQ). For affiliate links, "paid link" or "I earn a commission" beside the link; "affiliate link" alone is inadequate.
- **The brand must train and monitor.** Reasonable programs explain permitted claims and disclosure, check posts periodically, act on lapses, and keep checking for a few months after a deal ends. Outsourcing to an agency does not move the duty. Pre-approve paid posts, especially Stories.
- **Children:** disclosures may not work for young audiences at all; take extra care with creators whose audience is young.
- **Claims:** the brand is liable for what the creator claims. A creator cannot say what the brand could not prove.

## United States: the reviews rule

The Trade Regulation Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465, announced 14 August 2024, in force 21 October 2024) lets the FTC seek civil penalties, up to $53,088 per violation (the FTC's 2025 inflation adjustment, which it kept for 2026). It bans, where the business knew or should have known:

- Fake reviews and testimonials, including AI-generated ones and those from people with no real experience of the product.
- Paying or rewarding reviews conditioned on a positive (or negative) sentiment.
- Undisclosed reviews by officers, managers and staff, and soliciting them from relatives without disclosure.
- Company-controlled sites presented as independent reviewers.
- Suppressing negative reviews by threats or selective display.
- **Buying or selling fake indicators of social media influence** (bot or hijacked followers, views, likes) to misrepresent influence for a commercial purpose. This reaches a brand that inflates its own accounts and a creator who buys followers to sell sponsorships.

**Enforcement so far.** On 22 December 2025 the FTC sent warning letters to ten unnamed companies (paying staff to solicit five-star reviews, reviews from non-users), giving five business days to confirm compliance. In July 2026 it finalised its order against TruHeight (Vanilla Chip LLC): a $4M judgment suspended to $750,000 paid, over employee and vendor reviews, free products for five-star reviews and bot-run social profiles posing as real users (FTC press release, 15 July 2026; trade press reported it as one of the first actions under the rule, though the release itself cites no rule section). No case against an influencer for undisclosed posts under the new rule was found for this check.

## United Kingdom and the EU

- **UK:** the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 brought a fake-reviews ban and direct CMA enforcement with fines up to 10% of global turnover, in force from April 2025; the CMA published its fake-reviews guidance (CMA208) on 4 April 2025. The ASA's influencer guide (third edition, 2023) still applies: "#ad" or "Ad" up front, on every paid or gifted post, affiliate links included.
- **EU:** the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive requires commercial content to be identifiable. In the Commission's 2024 sweep of 576 influencers, 97% posted commercial content and only 20% disclosed it consistently (European Commission, 14 February 2024). France's 2023 influencer law adds its own labels and bans. Check the country before briefing.

## Platform tools

Turn them on in every paid or gifted post, and add the creator's own disclosure as well:

- **TikTok:** the content disclosure setting, which adds a "Paid partnership" or "Promotional content" label. Branded content must follow TikTok's branded content policy (restricted categories apply).
- **Instagram and Facebook:** the paid partnership label, tagging the brand as partner; partnership ads require it.
- **YouTube:** the "includes paid promotion" box in the video details, which shows a label at the start of the video; creators and brands stay responsible for legal compliance (YouTube help, checked October 2026).

## What the brief must say

- Every paid or gifted post uses the platform's label **and** says it is an ad: "ad" or "sponsored" at the front of the caption and said aloud in the first seconds of the video; "gifted by [brand]" for a gift.
- Affiliate links carry "paid link" or a commission line beside them.
- The creator makes only the claims the brief lists; no health, earnings or "best" claims the user cannot prove.
- Disclosure is a condition of payment, and the user will check the live post.

## What Manifold never does

Suggest a mention the creator will not disclose, offer payment for a positive review, buy or recommend buying followers, views or comments, or present a creator's paid video as an independent customer review.

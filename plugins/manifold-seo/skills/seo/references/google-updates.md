# What Google rewarded and punished, 2025 to 2026

The confirmed updates, what the best available evidence says they rewarded, and the policies that decide what not to build. Google publishes no winners and losers; every analysis below uses third-party visibility or traffic estimates, so read the patterns, not the percentages. Checked October 2026.

## What winners had

The one systematic study: Cyrus Shepard (Zyppy, April 2026) hand-classified over 400 sites that won or lost over twelve months spanning the December 2025 and March 2026 updates (Ahrefs traffic estimates, Spearman correlations).

| Trait | Winners | Losers |
|---|---|---|
| Offers a product or service | 70% | 35% |
| Lets the visitor complete a task (tool, calculator, booking, purchase) | 84% | 50% |
| Has proprietary assets (own data, tools, templates, images) | 93% | 57% |
| Tight topical focus | 76% | 61% |
| Strong brand | 33% | 16% |

- The traits stack: sites with four or five of them won about 69% of the time, sites with none 14%.
- **First-hand experience, user-generated content and "unique information" did not separate winners from losers** in this sample. Correlations are weak to moderate (0.2 to 0.4) and the sample started from sites already in the news, so this is evidence, not proof.
- Third-party write-ups of the March and May 2026 updates (vendor blogs, no shared method) agree in direction: original sources, established brands and first-hand expertise gained; thin aggregators, templated affiliate pages and scaled machine translations lost; UGC and forums lost some of the blanket lift they had in 2024. Use the direction, not their numbers.

What this means for a plan: a page that only summarises what already ranks is commodity content, which Google's own AI optimisation guide (July 2026) names as what adds little. The brief's angle should be an asset the site owns (data, a tool, a template, a test) or a task the page completes.

## Self-ranking listicles: contested

- **The case against.** Lily Ray (February 2026) showed seven SaaS sites losing 29% to 49% of Sistrix visibility from 21 January 2026, concentrated in blog, guide and tutorial folders that held tens to hundreds of "best X" lists ranking the publisher first, many retitled "2026" without new content, some scored as fully AI-written. She calls it a hypothesis; Google confirmed nothing. In September 2026 she found AI Overviews citing 38% fewer self-ranking lists than in June, and leaving out the brand a vendor ranked first 83% of the time when they did cite one.
- **The case for caution.** Kevin Indig (Growth Memo, August 2026; 60,000 queries, 5.32M results, SE Ranking data) found lists still in the top 10 for 55% of queries, the listicle share of position one down about four points since January, and 62% of vendor-written lists gaining estimated traffic (median 38%). Publisher lists beat vendor lists in 54% of head-to-heads. He could not tie the decline to self-promotion. Growth and Convert reported no crackdown across its clients.
- **What holds either way:** query wording decides the format (Google showed lists 4.5 times as often when the query asked for options), so a list belongs only on list-seeking queries. A list that ranks its author first with no testing, no criteria and a refreshed year is the pattern every loss shared. If the user publishes one, make it honest: real criteria, competitors that beat the user on some of them, and "how we tested". See [comparison pages](comparison-pages.md).

## Policies that decide what not to build

From Google's spam policies (updated 28 August 2026) and helpful content guidance (updated 5 October 2026):

- **Scaled content abuse** is many pages made mainly to rank, whatever made them: AI, templates, scraping, synonym rewriting, translation, or the same pages spread across sites. Method does not matter; value does. A page per keyword variant, city or competitor with swapped nouns is the pattern. The spam definition now also covers attempts to manipulate Google's generative AI answers.
- **Changing a page's date when the content has not substantially changed** is listed by Google as a sign of search-engine-first content, and was part of the January 2026 SaaS losses. Update the facts, examples and numbers, then the date.
- **No preferred word count.** Google says so in as many words. Length follows the topic; the winners' median is context, not a target.
- **Who, how, why.** Clear authorship, a stated method (for reviews: how many products, how tested, with evidence such as photos), and disclosure of AI use where readers would wonder.
- **Site reputation abuse** ("parasite SEO") is third-party content placed on a strong host mainly to borrow its ranking. Outside the EEA it draws a manual action on that section. Inside the EEA, since 30 August 2026 and under a European Commission mandate, Google applies no manual action; the section is instead ranked on its own, apart from the host. Buying placements on big sites to rank is a risk everywhere and worthless in the EEA.
- **Expired domain abuse** (repurposing a dropped domain's reputation) and **back button hijacking** (a malicious-practices policy added April 2026) are both demotion risks.

## Reading an update against the user's site

- A core update re-scores the whole site; recovery usually needs the next core update after the fix, months away. A spam update or manual action is about specific pages or practices; manual actions show in Search Console.
- Compare clicks, not impressions, around an update that falls near September 2025 ([Search Console](search-console.md#the-september-2025-break)).
- Many sites in one niche moving on the same days is an update; one site moving alone is more often technical or a competitor.
